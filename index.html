Policy Context · City of Missoula Housing Strategy
City of Missoula · Adopted 2019 · Updated 2026
A Place to Call Home: Meeting Missoula’s Housing Needs
Missoula’s first comprehensive, citywide housing strategy · Updated by City Council 10–1, February 2026
Read the Plan

Adopted in 2019 and updated in early 2026, A Place to Call Home is Missoula’s foundational housing strategy — a community-driven, 95-page framework built around four policy action areas: Fund, Preserve, Assist, and Build. City Housing Policy Specialist Parker Webb emphasized at the 2026 update that there is “no silver bullet” for the housing crisis — a principle this spatial analysis directly reinforces.

The plan’s Preserve pillar is the most direct mandate for this work. It calls for maintaining a live inventory of at-risk affordable properties, prioritizing anti-displacement strategies in upzoned areas, and partnering with nonprofits to acquire and protect naturally occurring affordable housing before market forces eliminate it. The city has already established an Affordable Housing Trust Fund to act on these goals — though the fund faced $1.5 million in requests against only $300,000 available in 2025, underscoring the urgency of targeting limited resources where displacement risk is highest.

This NOAH analysis operationalizes the plan’s preservation goals by answering a question the plan identifies but cannot answer alone: which specific properties, in which neighborhoods, are most at risk right now — and what intervention fits each context?

Spatial Distribution · NOAH on Low-Value Upzoned Land
City of Missoula Taxlot Dataset  ·  Land value ≤ $17/sq ft  ·  Multi-family & mobile home residential  ·  Zoning: Title 20 & Title 22 UDC (City of Missoula, 2026)  ·  Bubble size = unit count

When cities upzone low-value land, they create opportunity — and risk. These 184 properties holding 1,045 units sit at the intersection of both.

Methodology

Three layers intersected: a NOAH layer (multi-family & mobile homes built before 1985), a low land value filter (≤ $17/sq ft), and a derived upzone layer created by comparing Title 20 and Title 22 UDC (City of Missoula GIS, 2026) to identify parcels where zoning recently changed. Single-family homes excluded throughout. NOAH represents housing that functions as affordable today, without subsidy — upzoning appreciation can displace tenants long before new development is built.

Included in This Analysis · Mobile & Manufactured Housing

Mobile homes: included where most analyses stop short

Unlike most NOAH analyses, this dataset deliberately includes mobile and manufactured homes alongside multi-family properties — recognizing them as a critical and often overlooked segment of naturally occurring affordable housing. Missoula’s City Housing Director has called manufactured housing “the most threatened type of affordable housing” in the city due to redevelopment pressure, and the numbers bear that out: nearly 300 mobile homes were lost in Missoula County between 2011 and 2019 alone.

Unlike apartment buildings, mobile home residents often own their unit but rent the land beneath it — making them uniquely exposed when a park owner sells or redevelops. Recent examples include the closure of Skyview Trailer Park (2016) and ongoing lease instability at Travois Village (2025). Including them here ensures the map captures the full spectrum of displacement risk — not just the units that fit neatly into standard multi-family categories.

Why It Matters

Land ownership is the vulnerability

Most mobile home residents own their unit but lease the lot. When land values rise after upzoning, park owners face strong financial incentives to sell for redevelopment — leaving residents unable to move their home and with little legal recourse. Lot rent increases can also price out fixed-income residents before any redevelopment occurs.

Proven Intervention

Resident-Owned Communities (ROCs)

NeighborWorks Montana has pioneered a resident cooperative purchase model across the state. The City used $181,550 from the Affordable Housing Trust Fund to help residents of the Twen Tre park in Franklin to the Fort purchase their land in 2023 — a direct application of the A Place to Call Home Preserve strategy that kept 14+ households stably housed.

Recommended Next Step

Expand this analysis to park parcels

The Regional Housing Readiness Dashboard should incorporate a mobile home park parcel layer cross-referenced with ownership records and lot lease structures. This would identify parks most vulnerable to investor acquisition — particularly absentee or out-of-state owners — and prioritize them for proactive ROC outreach before displacement occurs.

Targeted Policy Recommendations

Neighborhood-tailored intervention strategies

01Westside

Nonprofit Acquisition Program

High unit density (avg. 10 units/property) makes Westside the most efficient neighborhood for Community Land Trust or nonprofit housing provider acquisitions — a direct application of the Plan’s Preserve action area and Affordable Housing Trust Fund resources.

02Franklin to the Fort

Neighborhood-Wide Anti-Displacement Zone

57 properties require a coordinated strategy aligned with the Plan’s call that “no neighborhood should be asked to experience radical change”: right of first refusal for tenants, proactive code assistance, and anti-speculation deed restrictions tied to upzone permits.

03Lewis & Clark

Small Landlord Incentive Program

With 20 properties averaging under 2 units, preservation requires reaching individual owners through the Plan’s Assist and Preserve tools — low-interest rehab loans, property tax deferrals, and affordable covenant incentives that make staying affordable more financially viable than selling.
